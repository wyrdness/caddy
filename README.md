## Overview

Cadborosaurus, commonly called 'Caddy', is a marine cryptid associated with the inland and coastal waters of the Pacific Northwest, particularly the Strait of Georgia, Cadboro Bay, and the waters around Vancouver Island. Witnesses describe a long-necked, serpentine animal with a horse- or camel-like head, vertical humps or coils behind the head, and either flippers or a fan-shaped tail; reported lengths range from approximately 5 m (15 ft) to over 30 m (100 ft) in the most extravagant accounts. The modern phenomenon coalesced in the autumn of 1933, when Major W. H. Langley, clerk of the British Columbia Legislature, reported a sighting from his sailboat near Discovery Island, off Cadboro Bay; the Victoria Daily Times' managing editor Archie Wills coined the name 'Cadborosaurus'. A purported juvenile carcass extracted in October 1937 from the stomach of a sperm whale processed at the Naden Harbour whaling station was photographed and tentatively examined before being lost; cetologists today usually attribute it to a known animal (most plausibly a fetal baleen whale or basking shark). Carcasses subsequently associated with Caddy have all been identified as known species (basking sharks, decomposed whales, oarfish, elephant seals). Indigenous Pacific Northwest Coast peoples maintain long-standing traditions of large sea creatures — including the Manhousat hiyitlik and the Kwakwaka'wakw sisiutl — that predate and remain conceptually distinct from the colonial-era Cadborosaurus framing. Cetacean biologists Edward L. Bousfield and Paul LeBlond compiled approximately 200 sightings and proposed the binomial 'Cadborosaurus willsi' in a 1995 paper, an attribution rejected by the mainstream zoological community. Skeptics, including Darren Naish, argue that the sightings record is best explained by a heterogeneous mix of misidentified known species rather than a single unknown animal.

## Etymology

- **Origin**: Coined by Archie Wills, managing editor of the Victoria Daily Times, in late 1933 following Major W. H. Langley's sighting near Discovery Island. Constructed from 'Cadboro' (Cadboro Bay, Victoria, B.C.) plus the Greek 'sauros' (lizard).
- **Meaning**: 'Cadboro Bay lizard' — applied by analogy with classical-zoology naming.
- **First use**: 1933, in the Victoria Daily Times.

## Notable Sightings

### Major W. H. Langley sighting (Discovery Island) ⭐
> **Date**: 1933-10-01  
> **Location**: Inshore waters near Discovery Island, off Cadboro Bay  
> **Witnesses**: 2 (Major W. H. Langley (clerk of the B.C. Legislature), Mrs. Langley)  
> **Evidence**: Newspaper coverage in the Victoria Daily Times  
Langley and his wife, sailing near Discovery Island, reported seeing the dome of a creature's back break the surface within roughly 30 m (100 ft) of their boat. The animal was described as the size of a large whale but unlike any known species, dark greenish-brown, with serrated marks along its back and sides. The Victoria Daily Times published the account under managing editor Archie Wills, who coined the name 'Cadborosaurus'.
**Credibility**: medium — Foundational modern Caddy report; the basis for the name.
**Sources**: Victoria Daily Times (October 1933); Bousfield, E. L. & LeBlond, P. H. (1995)

### Naden Harbour carcass ⭐
> **Date**: 1937-10-01  
> **Location**: Naden Harbour whaling station, Haida Gwaii  
> **Witnesses**: 4 (Whaling-station workers)

## Theories & Explanations

### Scientific

**Undescribed long-necked marine animal** _(Edward L. Bousfield, Paul H. LeBlond)_

Bousfield and LeBlond argue that the consistent long-necked, vertically-undulating profile across many independent witnesses points to an undescribed marine reptile or mammal.

### Skeptical

**Misidentification of basking shark and other large fish** _(Darren Naish, Mainstream marine zoologists)_

Many long-bodied surface sightings are best explained by basking sharks (Cetorhinus maximus), which can exceed 9 m (30 ft) and produce 'humped' wakes when surface-feeding.

**Decomposed whale or oarfish carcasses** _(Cetologists at Pacific Biological Station, Nanaimo)_

The 1937 Naden Harbour carcass and similar specimens are best explained by fetal baleen whales, partially-decomposed cetaceans, or oarfish (Regalecus glesne).

**Aggregated heterogeneous misidentifications** _(Darren Naish)_

Naish argues that the case is best explained by treating Caddy not as a single creature but as a category that pools different misidentified animals (sea lions in formation, elephant seals, basking sharks) under one cultural label.
